The tunnel is bored along the direction η = (−2/3, 1/3, 2/3), the axis Nieuwland's 1816 construction uses. This is fixed so the demo matches the paper's own optimal passage; only the inner cube's rotation and scale are yours to search over.

A convex shape is said to have Rupert's property if you can bore a tunnel through it and slide an identical copy of the shape through that tunnel. A cube has this property, which is surprising the first time you hear it: a hole through a cube that lets another whole cube pass through, without the two ever overlapping. The Nieuwland number of a shape is the largest scale factor ν for which a copy enlarged by that factor can still make the trip. For the cube, that number is 3√2/4, about 1.06066, found by Pieter Nieuwland in 1816 and still the largest known today.

The paper this widget accompanies gets at that number through polar duality, a way of turning a convex shape inside out: every vertex of the original becomes a face of the dual, and every face becomes a vertex. The authors show that if one shape passes through another in a particular way, the dual of the second shape passes through the dual of the first. Because the cube and octahedron are duals of each other, a passing certificate for one can be converted into a passing certificate for the other, which is how the paper proves they share the same Nieuwland number. Underneath, checking whether a passage is even possible comes down to a system of quadratic inequalities relating a rotation, a projection direction, and a translation, a shape that computer solvers can search directly.

To use the widget: drag the rotation sliders and watch the status pill above the buttons. Green means the current orientation of the inner cube fits entirely within the tunnel bored into the outer one; red means some corner pokes out. Push ν past 1.06066 and the slider turns red, since no orientation is known to work beyond Nieuwland's own limit. Click "Snap to Nieuwland's solution" to jump straight to his 1816 construction, and "Animate pass-through" to watch the inner cube slide the length of the tunnel at that orientation.
